Nature and Cognition: Methodological Approaches, New Discoveries, Emerging Perspectives on Human-Environment Interaction, and Future Research Directions

Organizer: Jason Watson, University of Colorado Denver, USA

Many studies have shown that interacting with nature may have beneficial effects for one's mental health. More specifically, interacting with nature may have dual benefits, both reducing stress and improving attentional control, defined as the ability to actively maintain task goals and avoid sources of interference, thereby representing a critical cornerstone of higher-order cognition. In recognition of the rapidly expanding body of empirical research exploring how nature influences attention, emotion, and other core cognitive processes, we recently convened the inaugural, biennial, affiliate meeting of the Society for Nature and Cognition (SNAC) here at the Annual Meeting of the Psychonomic Society. The theme of our first gathering, "New Pathways for Nature and Cognition," was designed to spotlight emerging perspectives, challenge prevailing paradigms, and inspire innovative directions in the interdisciplinary field of environment and cognition. This affiliate meeting was especially timely given the ongoing debate with respect to cognitive processes that are most likely to benefit from interacting with nature. One goal of the current symposium is to assemble expert speakers from SNAC to disseminate this "cutting-edge" work on human-environment interaction to the larger membership of the Psychonomic Society. A second goal is to potentially increase the number of our colleagues who are actively conducting empirical research on nature/cognition for the next affiliate meeting of SNAC in 2027 and beyond.

Natural Settings Buffer Against Process Losses in Group Creativity -- Ruth Ann Atchley, University of South Florida, USA

Collaboration often suffers from process losses such as production blocking and evaluation apprehension, suppressing idea quantity and diversity as groups work creatively. We assessed whether the cognitive benefits of natural environments extend to collaborative idea generation. Undergraduates (N = 272) were randomly assigned to work individually or in triads in an outdoor botanical garden or an indoor student center. After a 30-minute environmental exposure, participants completed the two creativity measures; group members also rated cohesion. Groups show higher creativity than individuals and outdoor exposure significantly improved AUT performance. While we did not see a difference in RAT performance comparing context conditions, group cohesion influences the nature effects on convergent creativity in groups. For RAT, cohesion negatively predicted scores indoors suggesting that group related process losses disrupted convergent creativity. In contrast, there was the opposite effect for outdoor groups. Group cohesion had a marginal positive relationship with RAT performance. This suggests that natural environments buffer the negative association between cohesion and convergent thinking, whereas built settings may exacerbate it.

High-Frequency Heart Rate Variability Mediates Attentional Control Benefits following Exposure to Nature Images -- Keith Hutchison, Montana State University, USA

Interactions with natural stimuli have a beneficial and restorative effect on a broad range of cognitive domains, promoting stress reduction and aiding mental recovery. However, the mechanisms by which these processes occur are not fully understood. Attention Restoration Theory (ART) predicts that an individual's directed attention is restored in nature whereas Stress Reduction Theory (SRT) predicts that nature soothes the physiological system, allowing for improved cognition. Within the framework of Jenkin's (1979) tetrahedral model, the current study focuses on attentional and physiological outcomes following nature exposure and how they relate to ART and SRT predictions. Specifically, participants had their heart rate variability (HRV) monitored while viewing images of nature or urban settings and then completed the antisaccade task. Results supported an indirect effect of nature viewing on attentional control, such that high frequency HRV fully mediating the relationship between the type of images viewed and later accuracy on the antisaccade task, providing support for SRT over ART.

Aging Naturally: Nature's effects on Executive Functioning and Stress Recovery in Older Adults -- Sara LoTemplio, University of Vermont, USA

Greater executive functioning (EF) protects against cognitive decline and higher heart rate variability (HRV) can facilitate recovery from stress—a key risk factor for accelerated cognitive aging. Therefore, EF and HRV play a key role in healthy aging. Previous research suggests that nature exposure can improve EF, HRV, and affect, but older adults are underrepresented in this work. In the current talk, we present two pilot studies aimed to examine the effect of nature on older adult's EF and HRV. In Study 1, we examine how contact with real-world nature (RWN) and virtual reality nature (VRN) affect healthy younger and older adults' EF, HRV, perceived stress, and affect. Participants (N = 62) were randomized into an RWN, VRN, or an indoor control condition, and outcomes were assessed before and after 20-minute exposure to environmental manipulation. Across both age groups, sitting indoors exacerbated perceived stress and total mood disturbance compared to RWN and VRN. There was also a marginally significant increase in forward digit span scores for VRN only in older adults. To expand on these findings, we conducted a second, higher powered (N = 80) study with older adults only (Study 2). Results from the studies will be presented.

Neural Mechanisms of Nature's Effects on Attention -- Amy McDonnell, Colorado State University, USA

Immersion in nature is believed to enhance attention, yet the neural mechanisms underlying these benefits remain unclear. This talk presents two studies examining how nature influences attention-related brain activity. In the first, 92 healthy adults were randomized to a 40-minute walk in nature or an urban environment. Before and after, we assessed performance on the Attention Network Task (ANT) and attention-related neural activity using scalp EEG. Results showed that attentional benefits were specific to executive control processes, reflected in modulation of the error-related negativity and frontal theta oscillations. In a follow-up study, 10 bedridden neurosurgical patients experienced virtual reality simulations of nature or urban environments. Before and after immersion, we measured ANT performance and attention-related brain activity using intracranial EEG. This provided recordings from subcortical structures and individual neurons, enabling direct characterization of neuronal firing dynamics not observable with scalp EEG. Preliminary findings localize nature-related effects to the anterior cingulate cortex. Together, these studies provide converging evidence on cortical and subcortical neural responses to nature.

The How and Who of Attention Restoration: Understanding Differences in Nature Benefits across Types of Measures and Populations -- Alexandre Marois, Université Laval, Canada

Nature exposure has long been shown to yield cognitive and mental health benefits. The main theoretical perspective, the Attention Restoration Theory (ART), posits that nature provides opportunities for directed attention to rest and recover given the involuntary -- and effortless -- attention capture elicited by its fascinating properties. This standpoint remains central in environmental psychology. However, recent empirical evidence has proposed alternative explanations regarding the role of directed attention. Many of these conclusions stem from cutting-edge research employing innovative and non-traditional experimental designs and measures. This presentation reports on two empirical studies examining the moderating role of outcomes and populations in attention restoration. A first experiment shows that objective and subjective measures of attention restoration may follow different pattern. We also present preliminary findings from a second study assessing patterns of attentional engagement and deployment across participants with varying levels of recreational specialization. Overall, this presentation underscores the importance of integrating emerging perspectives and novel paradigms to better understand nature's benefits.
